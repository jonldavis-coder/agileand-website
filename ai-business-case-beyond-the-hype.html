Building an AI business case that holds up

How to move from AI enthusiasm to a measured, owned business case, one use case at a time.

By the Agile& team · November 1, 2026

Why AI business cases fall apart

AI tools are easy to try and hard to turn into lasting value. Business cases often fail for familiar reasons: the use case was chosen because the technology was exciting rather than because the problem was important, nobody measured how the work performed before the change, and once the pilot ended no one owned the result.

A disciplined approach doesn't slow AI down. It makes sure the effort goes where it can matter, and that you can show what changed.

Define: start with the problem, not the tool

Write the problem in plain language and name who owns it. Describe the outcome you want, such as faster turnaround, fewer errors, or a better customer experience, and agree what's in and out of scope. A good test: could you explain why this matters without mentioning AI at all?

Measure: baseline before you build

Measure how the work performs today. Useful baselines include cycle time, cost or effort per item, error or rework rates, and customer effort or satisfaction. Keep it simple and repeatable. Without a baseline, any later claim of improvement is a guess.

Analyze: check fit, data, and risk

  • Root cause: is the delay or error really something AI can address, or is it a hand-off, policy, or data problem?
  • Data readiness: is the information the tool needs available, accurate, and allowed to be used?
  • Risk: what happens when the output is wrong, and who reviews it?
  • Adoption: what will change for the people doing the work?

The business case comes from this analysis: expected benefit against the baseline, the costs to build and run, the risks, and the assumptions you'll test in a pilot.

Improve: pilot small and compare

Run a short, time-boxed pilot with real users and real work. Measure the same things you baselined. Then make a clear decision: scale, adjust and re-test, or stop. Stopping a use case that doesn't deliver is a success of the process, not a failure.

Control: scale, own, and sustain

When a pilot proves its value, scale it deliberately. Assign an owner, monitor quality and benefits over time, keep guardrails current, and update training. Then reinvest what you learned in the next use case.
